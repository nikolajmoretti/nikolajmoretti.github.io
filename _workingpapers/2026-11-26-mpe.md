**Abstract**: Static models of tax competition suggest that a minimum tax can make even low-tax jurisdictions better off, so that the global minimum tax of 15% on multinational firms' profits would be in tax havens' own interest. This article asks whether this holds when governments are forward-looking. It analyzes Markov perfect equilibria of a dynamic game of tax competition between a high-tax and a low-tax player with asynchronous, stochastic moves, in which tax rates are strategic complements. In this setting, each player sets its rate above its static best response to pull the opponent's future rate up. A binding minimum tax can make the low-tax player unresponsive, thus removing the high tax player's wedge. If the minimum tax is below a threshold, the high-tax player lowers its rate despite strategic complementarity. While the high-tax player gains in steady-state returns, the low-tax player loses for minimum taxes just above the rate it sets without one. Calibrated to business tax competition between German municipalities around the introduction of a minimum tax in 2004, the model produces a cut in the high-tax player's rate close to a recent causal estimate, the low-tax player loses for every minimum tax that binds it alone, and aggregate tax revenue increases unless the minimum tax lies just above the low-tax player's rate without one. If these results carry over to the international setting, havens are less likely to gain from a minimum tax than static models suggest, which makes their participation in the global minimum tax harder to secure.
